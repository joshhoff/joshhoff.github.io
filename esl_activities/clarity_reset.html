3-Day Potential Reset Sheet

Break the freeze, lower overwhelm, and build momentum

Day 1 – Clear the Mental Clutter

Morning (20 min)
  • Mind dump: Write down every task, idea, and worry — no organizing yet.
  • Circle only 3 things that would make today feel like a win.
Midday (5–10 min)
  • Box breathing (inhale 4, hold 4, exhale 4, hold 4).
  • Do one 2-minute task from your list immediately.
Evening (10 min)
  • Write down 3 things you did today that mattered (big or small).
  • Remove one distraction trigger from your environment for tomorrow (e.g., phone charging station outside bedroom, log out of a distracting app).

Day 2 – Prime for Focus

Morning (15–20 min)
  • Before checking your phone: drink water, move your body for 5 minutes (stretch, walk, etc.), then do your starter ritual (candle + playlist, special pen + notebook, or similar).
  • Start with your most mentally important task first — even for 15 minutes.
Midday (10 min)
  • Switch location for a burst of novelty (different chair, cafe, standing desk).
  • Use the “just start” rule: commit to 5 minutes of your next task — you can quit after that (but you probably won’t).
Evening (10–15 min)
  • Quick tidy of your workspace — make it ready for tomorrow.
  • Write tomorrow’s first action on a sticky note and leave it in view.

Day 3 – Build the Feedback Loop

Morning (15 min)
  • Do your starter ritual.
  • Begin with yesterday’s carry-over or the task from your sticky note.
  • Set a timer for 25 minutes, then take a 5-minute break. Repeat once.
Midday (5–10 min)
  • Micro-reflection: What’s working? What’s still sticky? What’s one small thing I can change now?
  • Do one small improvement to your setup or workflow.
Evening (10 min)
  • Review your Day 1 mind dump. Cross out anything irrelevant or done.
  • Pick 3 next steps for tomorrow.

Notes
• Don’t wait for motivation — use structure to start; let momentum create motivation.
• Track progress visibly — even a sticky-note wall of completed tasks can boost drive.
• Self-compassion is fuel — harsh self-talk often kills focus faster than distraction.
